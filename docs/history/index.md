# History: from neuroinformatics to brain foundation models

Oct 27, 2026 · @Laura M

Over three decades, about 30 major initiatives have tried to pool brain data and build a shared model of the brain; as of October 2026, most survive as permanent archives, atlases and AI models rather than as the original megaprojects.

This document covers national brain initiatives, mapping consortia, data archives and standards, and the newest brain foundation models. Each entry gives what it proposed, what it built, and its current status. The [master list](master-list.md) summarizes all 33 in one table. Every archive, atlas or portal named here that you can actually download data from has a full entry with species, technique and access terms in the [resource list](../resources/index.md).

Three things stand out:

- **The data layer works.** Open archives (OpenNeuro, DANDI, EBRAINS) and shared formats (BIDS, NWB) are active and growing, and now supply training data for AI models.
- **The megaprojects have mostly converted.** The EU Human Brain Project became EBRAINS; Blue Brain became the Open Brain Institute; Japan's Brain/MINDS became Brain/MINDS 2.0.
- **The money is moving.** US BRAIN funding is down from its 2023 peak and faces a cliff after FY2026, while China and Japan are expanding; Canada and Australia still have no dedicated national program.

The field moved through four overlapping waves: shared databases (1990s), whole-brain atlases (2000s), national "moonshot" initiatives (2013–2018), and, since about 2020, open data standards plus AI models trained across many datasets.

**Wave 1 — Neuroinformatics (1993–2005).** The first wave was about making existing data usable by someone who had not collected it. The response was infrastructure including databases, catalogues, and the committees needed to agree on shared formats, common vocabulary, and a way to cite a dataset. The [US Human Brain Project](national-initiatives.md) (NIMH and partners, 1993–2004) funded that first
generation of tools, and an OECD push for international coordination produced the [INCF](national-initiatives.md) in Stockholm in 2005, which still runs the process by which BIDS and NWB are endorsed. [ModelDB](../resources/models.md), started at Yale's SenseLab in 1996, is still the main archive of published computational neuroscience models.

**Wave 2 — Atlases and connectomes (2003–2012).** Paul Allen founded the Allen Institute for Brain Science in 2003; its Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (2006) showed that an industrial-scale, fully open brain dataset could work. The Blue Brain Project (École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne \[EPFL\], 2005) set out to simulate cortex in biological detail. The NIH Human Connectome Project (2009–2010 start) mapped human brain wiring with MRI and released it openly. UK Biobank began brain imaging of 100,000 people in 2014.

**Wave 3 — National brain initiatives (2013–2018).** In 2013 the EU launched the Human Brain Project (HBP, €1 billion-scale, 10 years) and the US launched the BRAIN Initiative. Japan (Brain/MINDS, 2014), Korea (2016), China (China Brain Project, approved 2016, started \~2021), Australia (2016 proposal) and Canada (Canadian Brain Research Strategy \[CBRS\], from 2015) followed. In 2017–2018 these programs formed the International Brain Initiative (IBI) to coordinate data sharing and ethics.

**Wave 4 — Open standards, cell atlases and AI (2017–now).** Common formats (Brain Imaging Data Structure \[BIDS\], 2016; Neurodata Without Borders \[NWB\]) and archives (OpenNeuro, DANDI) made data reusable. BRAIN Initiative's cell-census networks (BICCN, then BICAN) produced near-complete cell-type atlases of mouse and human brain. Electron-microscopy connectomes (MICrONS, the fly connectome FlyWire) reached whole-brain or cubic-millimetre scale. Since 2023 groups have trained "foundation models" of neural activity on these pooled archives — the newest attempt at a unified model of the brain. The HBP ended in 2023 (succeeded by EBRAINS) and Blue Brain closed in 2024 (succeeded by the Open Brain Institute), marking a shift from one-off megaprojects to permanent infrastructure.
